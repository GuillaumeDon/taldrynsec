01 — What Is Defensive Security?

The Blue Team Perspective

Defensive Security focuses on understanding what needs to be protected and implementing security measures to prevent, detect, and mitigate the impact of potential attacks. The goal is to be prepared for incidents and respond when they occur.

Defenders — often called the Blue Team — need to understand how attackers think, what they target, and how attacks typically unfold. By identifying critical infrastructure, understanding attackers' goals, and applying defences, defenders help protect their clients' systems.

Defensive security aligns directly with the CIA Triad: defenders work to ensure systems remain available and protected, maintaining confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

The 5 Core Defensive Concepts

🛡️
Prevention
Putting security controls in place to stop attacks before they happen — firewalls, antivirus, patching, access controls.
👁️
Detection
Monitoring systems and networks to identify suspicious or malicious activity through logs, alerts, and security tools.
🚨
Mitigation
Taking action during an incident to limit damage — blocking traffic, isolating affected systems, disabling compromised accounts.
🔬
Analysis
Investigating what happened and how systems were affected — reviewing logs, evidence, and other artefacts.
🔄
Response & Improvement
Recovering from the incident and improving defences to reduce the risk of similar attacks in the future.
Key Takeaway

Defensive security is not just about stopping attacks — it's about visibility, preparedness, and continuous improvement. You can't defend what you can't see, and you can't improve without analysing what went wrong.

02 — Understanding Your Environment

The City Analogy

Think of your client's infrastructure as a bustling city. Like city guards, you need to know the layout, watch for trouble, and know how to respond to keep it safe.

Before you can defend anything, you must understand what exists, what it does, and how it fits into the overall environment. Defenders are not responsible for protecting everything on the internet — only what belongs to their organisation or client.

Key Takeaway

Visibility first. You cannot defend what you cannot see. Map your client's infrastructure before applying any controls. Know every asset, its purpose, and its exposure. The city analogy: a guard who doesn't know the city layout cannot protect it.

03 — Defending Your Environment

The Defender Mindset

Your success as a defender relies on more than just knowing your environment. You must understand the attackers who want to break into your systems. Attackers rarely target a single system — they compromise one asset and pivot to the next, building toward their goal.

Example attack chain: malicious email → compromised employee workstation → stolen credentials → access to mail server → sensitive data on database server. Each step is an opportunity to apply a security control.

Key Defender Principles

Threat Anticipation

Review the systems you protect and ask "What if?" Imagine realistic paths an attacker may take to achieve their goal.

Attack Awareness

Attacks typically follow recognisable stages. Studying common attack chains and frameworks (e.g. MITRE ATT&CK) is incredibly useful for defenders.

Risk Prioritisation

Not every part of your system carries equal risk. Identify high-value systems and targets — protect the crown jewels first.

Continuous Adaptation

Defence is not a one-time setup. Threats evolve, techniques change, and new vulnerabilities emerge. Defenders must keep up.

Infrastructure → Threats → Defences

ComponentWhat Could Go WrongDefences
Employee Devices Someone clicks a bad link or downloads unsafe software Antivirus to detect bad programs · Regular software updates
Web Server Attackers try to break into the website Only allow safe traffic · Use secure communication (HTTPS)
Mail Server Malicious or deceptive emails (phishing) Spam filters · Scan attachments
Firewall Strangers from the internet try to break in Firewall rules that control access · Block known troublemakers
The Outside Internet External threats come from here Restrict inbound traffic · Monitor for suspicious activity

No single tool stops every bad attempt. Using a layered set of defences makes it much harder for attackers to succeed — this is called defence in depth.

Key Takeaway

Defence in depth: no single control is sufficient. Layer protections across every component. Think like an attacker to anticipate their path, then place controls at each step. The goal: make each stage of an attack chain costly and detectable.

Vocabulary

Key Terms

  • Defensive Security — Understanding what needs to be protected and implementing measures to prevent, detect, and mitigate the impact of attacks.
  • Blue Team — A group of cyber security defenders tasked with protecting systems and responding to threats.
  • Client Infrastructure — The networks, servers, devices, and applications belonging to an organisation that need protection.
  • Visibility — The ability to see and monitor activity across systems to spot potential issues. "You can't defend what you can't see."
  • Threat — A potential danger, such as a hacker or malware, that could harm systems or data.
  • Risk — The likelihood and potential impact of a threat successfully harming an organisation.
  • Prevention — Stopping threats before they can cause harm by blocking, restricting, or reducing opportunities for attack.
  • Detection — The process of identifying threats or suspicious activity in networks and systems.
  • Mitigation — Actions taken to reduce or stop the impact of a threat once it has been identified.
  • Analysis — Investigating what happened, how systems were affected, and what was impacted — by reviewing logs and evidence.
  • Defence in Depth — A layered security approach where multiple overlapping controls make it harder for attackers to succeed even if one control fails.
  • Threat Anticipation — Proactively imagining realistic attack paths to apply controls before an incident occurs.
  • Attack Chain / Pivot — Attackers rarely stop at one system — they compromise one asset and pivot to the next, building toward a final goal.
  • SOC (Security Operations Centre) — A team that monitors networks and systems to detect and investigate suspicious activity.
  • Threat Intelligence — Research into current threats, attackers, and trends to help organisations prepare defences.
  • DFIR (Digital Forensics & Incident Response) — Investigating security incidents to understand how an attack happened and contain the threat.

Prevention → Detection → Mitigation → Analysis → Response & Improvement.
What is the goal when a defender puts security controls in place to stop threats before damage occurs?
Prevention.
What process involves reviewing logs and evidence to understand how an incident happened?
Analysis.
What is visibility in defensive security? Why does it matter?
The ability to see and monitor activity across systems to spot potential issues. "You can't defend what you can't see." Without visibility, defenders have no way to detect or respond to threats.
In the city analogy, what does a Firewall represent?
The city gate — it controls what traffic is allowed in or out of the network, just like a gate controls who enters or exits a city.
What does "the Internet" represent in the city analogy?
Everything outside the city walls — external networks not controlled by the organisation, where most threats originate.
What should a defender do to Employee Devices to protect against malware downloads?
Install antivirus to detect bad programs and apply regular software updates to patch known vulnerabilities.
What defender principle focuses on identifying the most critical systems to guide security efforts?
Risk Prioritisation — not every part of a system carries equal risk. Defenders should identify and prioritise high-value systems and targets.
What is defence in depth?
A layered security approach where multiple overlapping controls are applied. If one control fails, others are still in place — making it much harder for an attacker to fully succeed.
Why do attackers rarely stop at the first system they compromise?
They use it as a stepping stone to pivot to other systems, escalating access toward their goal (e.g. employee device → mail server → database with sensitive data).
What is Threat Anticipation? How does it differ from Detection?
Threat Anticipation is proactively imagining attack paths before an incident occurs. Detection is identifying suspicious activity while it is happening (or has happened).
What does a SOC do?
A Security Operations Centre monitors networks and systems to detect and investigate suspicious activity or security alerts in real time.
What is DFIR?
Digital Forensics & Incident Response — investigating security incidents to understand how an attack happened, containing the threat, and restoring affected systems.
What is Threat Intelligence?
Research into current threats, attacker groups, techniques, and trends to help organisations prepare and defend against potential attacks before they happen.
